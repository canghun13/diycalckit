How Much Baseboard Do I Need? Room & Whole-House Guide

July 24, 2026 · DIYCalcKit

Baseboard is sold and installed by the linear foot, so the whole estimate comes down to one number: the perimeter of every room you're trimming, adjusted for doorways and waste. Get that number right and the board count and cost follow automatically.

Step 1: Measure the Perimeter, Not the Floor Area

Add up all four wall lengths in each room — this is your baseboard run before any adjustments. A common mistake is estimating from square footage instead; a 120 sq ft room could be a long narrow 8x15 or a near-square 11x11, and those two shapes need very different amounts of baseboard even though the floor area matches.

Room SizeApprox. PerimeterBaseboard After 1 Door + 15% Waste
10 x 10 ft40 ft~42 ft
12 x 14 ft52 ft~56 ft
14 x 16 ft60 ft~65 ft
1,500 sq ft whole house~400-460 ft (varies by layout)~460-530 ft

Step 2: Subtract for Doorways

Baseboard stops at the door casing rather than running across the opening, so subtract roughly 3 feet of baseboard for each doorway in the room. This is easy to forget when you're totaling up a whole house with a dozen door openings — it adds up to several extra boards if skipped.

Step 3: Add a Waste Allowance

Inside and outside corners require miter or coped cuts that use more material than a straight run. Budget 10% extra for a simple rectangular room, 15% as a solid default for most homes, and 20% if the room has lots of corners and angles or you're new to cutting trim.

Step 4: Add Door and Window Casing (If You're Replacing It)

OpeningTypical Casing Footage
Standard interior door (one side)~17 linear feet
Standard window~10 linear feet

A door needs casing on both sides of the wall if both rooms are getting new trim — double the per-door figure if you're finishing both faces of the opening.

Step 5: Decide on Crown Molding Separately

Crown molding runs the full ceiling perimeter with no door deduction, since it continues uninterrupted above every opening. Its compound miter cuts at each corner also waste more material than baseboard's simpler 45-degree cuts, so budget a few extra percent of waste on top of your standard allowance if you're adding crown to the same room.

Step 6: Convert to Boards

Trim stock typically comes in 8, 10, 12, and 16-foot lengths. Divide your total linear footage by your chosen board length and round up — a partial board still counts as a full purchase. Matching board length to your longest wall minimizes both seams and waste; a 12-ft wall with 12-ft stock needs no splice at all.

What Height Baseboard Should You Buy?

Linear footage tells you how much to buy; profile height tells you which one. Height is the decision that makes a room look either deliberately trimmed or slightly off, and it scales with ceiling height rather than with room size. Material prices and a cost estimate for each option are on the baseboard & trim calculator.

Ceiling heightTypical baseboard heightNotes
8 ft3 to 4 inAnything taller starts to crowd a standard wall
9 ft5 to 6 inThe most common upgrade point in newer builds
10 ft and up7 in and upOften paired with taller door casing to stay in proportion

A useful sanity check: baseboard usually looks right at roughly 1/18 to 1/16 of the ceiling height. There is one hard constraint that overrides taste. Your baseboard must be at least as tall as the door casing is wide at the bottom, or the baseboard will run past the casing edge and leave a visible step. Measure the casing before you commit to a height, not after.

Coping vs. Mitering Inside Corners

Every inside corner is either mitered (two 45-degree cuts meeting) or coped (one board runs to the wall, the second is cut to the profile of the first). This choice changes your material order, so it belongs in the estimate rather than being decided at the saw.

MiterCope
SpeedFast, both cuts on the sawSlower, coping saw or jigsaw per joint
Out-of-square wallsOpens into a visible gapStays tight, absorbs the error
Seasonal movementJoint opens as wood shrinksJoint stays closed
Extra materialMinimalAdd a few inches per coped joint for practice cuts

Practically: cope inside corners in older homes, where almost no corner is a true 90 degrees, and miter outside corners always. If you plan to cope, add a little on top of the usual 10 to 15% allowance, because the first coped joint on any profile is normally a throwaway.

For the ordering errors that cause most over- and under-buys, including the door deduction, perimeter-versus-floor-area confusion, and keeping base shoe out of the baseboard total, see the checklist on the baseboard & trim calculator.

Pro tip: Order slightly more than your calculated total and keep the leftover pieces. A future repair or a scratched section is a much easier fix with matching material already in the garage than a return trip to match a discontinued profile.

Frequently Asked Questions

What length boards should I buy: 8, 12, or 16 feet?

Match the stock length to your longest wall so that wall can be run in a single piece. A 12-foot wall done with 8-foot boards needs a scarf joint, which is both more visible and more waste. Longer boards cost more per piece and are awkward to transport and cut, so the usual approach is to buy longer stock only for the walls that need it and shorter stock for everything else.

Do I need base shoe or quarter round?

Only if the gap between the baseboard and the floor is visible. New flooring installed with an expansion gap almost always needs it. If you are installing baseboard after the flooring and the floor is flat, a taller baseboard set tight to the surface can skip it. Base shoe nails into the floor rather than the baseboard, so it is ordered and calculated as a separate linear footage.

Should baseboard go in before or after flooring?

Flooring first is the more forgiving order for most DIY jobs, because the baseboard then covers the expansion gap and you avoid undercutting or scribing. Carpet is the exception: baseboard goes in first and is held up slightly so the carpet and pad tuck underneath. If the baseboard is already installed and you are adding hard flooring, base shoe covers the gap without removing anything.

How much extra should I order for a whole-house trim job?

Ten to fifteen percent over your calculated linear footage is the normal allowance, and the upper end is the safer choice on a whole-house job with many corners or a coped installation. Keep the offcuts rather than returning surplus. Trim profiles are discontinued often enough that matching a scratched section years later is far harder than storing a few spare pieces.
