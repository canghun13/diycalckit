How Much Tile Do I Need for a Backsplash? Complete Guide

July 24, 2026 · DIYCalcKit

A backsplash is one of the smallest tile jobs in the house, but it's also one of the most visible — every cut, every seam, and every shade mismatch sits right at eye level. Getting the quantity right the first time means one supply run instead of two, and buying from a single dye lot instead of a mismatched patch.

Step 1: Measure Width and Height

Measure the total width of counter you're tiling above, then the height from the countertop to the bottom of the upper cabinets. Standard kitchen backsplash height is 18 inches; a full-height backsplash running to the ceiling can be 24 to 36+ inches depending on your cabinet layout. Multiply width by height (in feet) for your gross square footage.

Counter Width18" Height24" Height
8 ft12 sq ft16 sq ft

Step 2: Subtract Large Openings

Subtract large openings like a range hood or window from your gross area — but leave standard outlets alone. Your waste allowance already covers the small cuts needed around them, and subtracting every outlet individually just adds unnecessary math for a negligible difference in tile count.

Step 3: Add Waste, Then Round Up to Whole Boxes

Waste allowance runs from about 10% for a straight-stacked layout up to roughly 20% for herringbone, because intricate patterns turn more of every tile into an unusable offcut. The backsplash tile calculator applies the right percentage for each pattern automatically, so this guide focuses on the part most people get wrong after the waste math: tile is not sold by the square foot.

Showrooms and big-box stores quote a price per square foot, but you buy in sealed boxes. A 14 sq ft job does not mean you buy 14 sq ft. You buy however many full boxes cover 14 sq ft, and the leftover comes home with you. That rounding is often larger than the waste allowance itself.

Tile formatTypical box coverageBoxes for a 15 sq ft backsplash (+12% waste)
3x6 subway (loose tile)~10 sq ft (80 pcs)2 boxes = 20 sq ft
4x12 subway~11 sq ft2 boxes = 22 sq ft
Mosaic on 12x12 mesh sheets~10 sheets = 10 sq ft2 boxes = 20 sq ft
Large format 12x24~16 sq ft (8 pcs)2 boxes = 32 sq ft

Two practical consequences. First, on a small backsplash the box rounding usually hands you your attic spares for free, so you rarely need to add a separate "extra box for repairs" on top. Second, large-format tile is the worst fit for a small backsplash precisely because one box overshoots the job so badly. If you only need 15 sq ft, a smaller format wastes far less money even though its waste percentage looks similar.

Step 4: Convert Area to Tile Count

Find the area of a single tile in square feet (length × width in inches, divided by 144), then divide your total area (with waste already added) by that number and round up. A 3"x6" subway tile covers 0.125 sq ft, so a 16.5 sq ft backsplash needs about 132 tiles.

Grout: A Much Smaller Job Than It Looks

Backsplash grout needs are small compared to flooring — most standard kitchen backsplashes use well under one full bag. For an exact bag count based on your specific tile size and joint width, use a dedicated grout calculator rather than eyeballing it from the backsplash area alone.

Pro tip: Buy all your tile in one order from the same dye lot. Reordering later — even the "same" tile — often comes from a different production batch with a slightly different shade, which is very visible on a backsplash at eye level.

How High Should the Backsplash Go?

Height is the single biggest lever on your tile order, and it is a decision you make before any calculator is useful. The standard countertop-to-upper-cabinet gap is 18 inches, but that is a cabinetry convention, not a rule you have to follow.

Height choiceTile for 10 ft of counterBest when
Standard 18 in gap~15 sq ftBudget jobs, busy patterned tile, rental turnovers
Full height behind the range only~19 sq ftYou want a focal point without tiling the whole kitchen
Counter to ceiling, one wall~40-50 sq ftOpen shelving, no uppers, or a range wall with a hood

Two things swing this decision more than looks. A range wall with a chimney-style hood almost always reads better tiled to the ceiling, because stopping the tile mid-wall leaves an awkward band of paint framing the hood. And if you are tiling over an existing painted wall with no uppers, going to the ceiling avoids a horizontal termination line that needs trim: the ceiling becomes the edge.

Where you stop also changes what you buy. A run that ends in open air needs bullnose or metal edge trim along that edge; a run that dies into a cabinet or the ceiling does not. Trim is sold per piece, so measure the exposed edges in linear feet before you order.

For the ordering mistakes that cause most over- and under-buys (pattern waste, dye lots, forgotten cutouts, outlet placement, and edge trim), see the checklist on the backsplash tile calculator.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many boxes of tile is a typical backsplash?

Most kitchen backsplashes land on two boxes. A standard 18-inch-high run over 10 feet of counter needs roughly 15 sq ft plus waste, and common formats such as 3x6 subway or mesh-mounted mosaic cover about 10 to 11 sq ft per box. Because you can only buy whole boxes, that rounding usually gives you enough leftover for future repairs without ordering an extra box.

Is mosaic sheet tile counted differently from loose tile?

Yes. Mesh-mounted mosaic is sold and installed by the sheet, not by the individual chip, so you count sheets rather than tiles. Most sheets are 12x12 inches, which means one sheet covers about one square foot and the sheet count is close to your square footage plus waste. Partial sheets can be cut apart and the individual pieces reused along edges, which is why mosaic often wastes less than its stated percentage suggests.

Can I tile a backsplash directly over painted drywall?

Usually yes, if the paint is sound and the wall is flat. Scuff-sand glossy paint so the thinset or mastic can grip, and repair any loose or peeling areas first. Skim-coating is only necessary if the wall is uneven enough that large-format tile would rock. Behind a sink or a cooktop where splashing is constant, a cement backer board is the safer substrate.

Should I tile behind the range to the ceiling or stop at cabinet height?

It depends on the hood. A chimney-style hood with open wall on both sides almost always looks better with tile running to the ceiling, because stopping partway leaves a band of paint framing the hood. If the range sits under a microwave or a cabinet, matching the standard 18-inch height across the whole kitchen keeps the run consistent and costs less.
