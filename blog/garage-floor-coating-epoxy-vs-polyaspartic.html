Garage Floor Paint vs. Epoxy vs. Polyaspartic: Which One Your Slab Can Actually Hold

Updated August 2026 · 8 min read

Search this question and almost every result is written by a company that installs one of these coatings. The conclusion is usually the same: whichever product that company sells wins, and DIY fails. That framing skips the part that actually decides the outcome. Before any of these three products is the right answer, your slab has to be capable of holding a coating at all, and that is something you can test yourself in an afternoon with a roll of plastic sheeting and a bottle of water.

Test the Slab First. Everything Else Is Secondary

Coating failures on garage floors are overwhelmingly bond failures, not product failures. Two conditions cause most of them, and both are testable before you spend anything.

The moisture test. Concrete slabs sit on soil, and many older ones were poured without a vapor barrier underneath. Moisture rising through the slab pushes any film coating off from below, and no amount of surface prep changes that. Tape a two-foot square of clear plastic sheeting to the floor, sealing all four edges completely, and leave it for 24 to 48 hours. If you find condensation on the underside of the plastic or a darkened patch of concrete beneath it, you have vapor drive. Do that test in more than one spot, including near the garage door and along any wall shared with soil. A slab that fails this test needs a moisture-tolerant system, not a hardware store kit.

The absorption test. Sprinkle water on several areas of bare concrete. If it soaks in and darkens the surface within a minute or so, the slab is open and will accept a coating. If it beads and sits there, the concrete is sealed, densified, or contaminated with oil, and the coating will sit on top of that layer rather than bonding to concrete. Sealed slabs have to be mechanically ground, which is the single most common reason a DIY job peels a year later.

One more constraint that is easy to miss: a newly poured slab needs roughly 28 days to cure before it takes any coating. New builds and new garage additions fail on this more often than old floors do.

Hot Tire Pickup, and Why It Only Happens to Some Floors

Warm tires soften a coating and stick to it; as the car cools the tire grips harder than the coating grips the concrete, and a patch lifts in the shape of the tire contact patch. It is the signature garage floor failure and it looks like the product was defective.

It is almost always a bond or thickness problem rather than a product problem. A thin single coat of paint has neither the adhesion nor the film build to resist it. A properly prepped epoxy at full thickness usually survives it. Polyaspartic bonds deeper into the concrete and stays slightly flexible, which is why it rarely shows the failure. If you park a daily driver in a hot climate, weight this factor heavily. If the garage stores tools and bikes and the car lives outside, it barely matters and you can buy much further down the price list.

When Each One Is the Right Answer

  • Floor paint makes sense for a storage-only garage, a workshop floor you expect to re-coat, a rental you do not own long-term, or a basement floor that never sees a vehicle. It is honest about what it is: a cosmetic upgrade with mild dust control.
  • Epoxy is the sweet spot for most owner-occupied garages where you are willing to do the prep properly. Two-part kits are widely available, the working time is long enough to learn on, and a well-prepped epoxy floor with a topcoat lasts most of a decade. Buy the kit with the highest solids content you can find, and treat the included etching acid as a minimum rather than a substitute for grinding.
  • Polyaspartic is the durability answer, and it is also the one most likely to defeat a first-time DIYer. The pot life can be under 20 minutes, which means you commit to a section and cannot stop to fix a mistake. It also tolerates humidity and cooler temperatures better than epoxy, which is why professional crews finish a garage in a day. If you want this system and have not coated a floor before, price the installed version rather than the kit.

A note on the word "epoxy" on a label. A one-part epoxy paint is latex acrylic with a small amount of epoxy resin in it. It performs like paint, not like a two-part epoxy system. If the can does not come with a separate hardener you mix in, it belongs in the first column of the table above regardless of what the front of the can says.

Ordering: What Coverage Numbers Actually Mean

Coating kits state coverage in square feet, and that figure assumes a smooth, steel-troweled slab. Garage floors rarely are. A broom finish, light spalling, or a pitted surface all increase the real surface area, and the coating fills that texture before it builds any film thickness.

Two practical consequences. Buy the material for the whole floor in one purchase so every batch comes from the same lot, the same way you would with tile or grout. And measure the floor rather than trusting the "2-car garage" label on the kit, because a two-car garage ranges from roughly 400 to over 600 square feet. Common Mistakes

  • Skipping the moisture test. The most expensive mistake on this page, because it is the one that ruins a correctly applied coating.
  • Treating acid etching as full prep. Etching opens a light broom finish; it does not remove a sealer or years of soaked-in oil, and it adds water to the slab that then has to dry out.
  • Coating over oil stains. Degrease first and re-test with water. Nothing bonds to oil.
  • Parking too early. Foot traffic and vehicle traffic have very different cure requirements. Walking on it at 24 hours does not mean it will take tires.
  • Ignoring the temperature window. Most kits need the slab, not just the air, within a stated range. An unheated garage in early spring can have a slab well below the air temperature.
  • Coating the whole floor when the drive lane is the problem. If only the tire tracks are stained, a chip-flake system in the drive lane can be a fraction of the cost of a full coating.

The Bottom Line

Run the plastic sheet test and the water test before you compare products. A slab with vapor drive rules out ordinary film coatings no matter which one you prefer, and a sealed slab means grinding is not optional. Once the slab passes, match the product to the use: paint for storage and workshops, a properly prepped two-part epoxy for most garages that park a car, and polyaspartic when you want to do this once and never again, budgeting for professional installation unless you have coated a floor before.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I put epoxy over a painted garage floor?

Not reliably. The epoxy will bond to the paint rather than to the concrete, so the assembly is only as strong as the paint underneath, and old floor paint is usually the weakest layer present. Grinding the paint off is the standard answer. If you want to try, test a small area first by coating it, letting it cure fully, and then attempting to pry it up with a scraper at an edge.

How do I know if my garage floor is sealed?

Sprinkle water on several spots of bare concrete. If it soaks in and darkens the surface within about a minute, the slab is open and will accept a coating. If it beads up and sits on the surface, there is a sealer, a densifier, or contamination in the way, and the floor needs mechanical grinding before anything will bond to it.

Is polyaspartic worth the extra cost over epoxy?

It depends on how long you intend to keep the floor and who is applying it. Polyaspartic lasts substantially longer, resists hot tire pickup, and does not yellow in sunlight, so over fifteen years it is often cheaper per year than re-coating epoxy twice. As a DIY project it is much less forgiving, because the pot life can be under twenty minutes and there is no opportunity to stop mid-section and correct a mistake.

How long does a garage floor need to cure before coating?

A newly poured slab needs roughly 28 days before it takes any coating, because fresh concrete is both damp and highly alkaline. This catches people in new construction more often than in older homes. For an existing slab, the relevant waiting period is after washing or acid etching, since the concrete has to dry fully before the coating goes down.

What causes garage floor coating to peel where the car parks?

That pattern is hot tire pickup. Warm tires soften the coating and adhere to it, and as they cool the tire grips more strongly than the coating grips the concrete, lifting a patch shaped like the tire contact area. It is a symptom of inadequate surface prep or insufficient film thickness rather than a defective product, which is why it shows up in the drive lane and nowhere else on the same floor.
