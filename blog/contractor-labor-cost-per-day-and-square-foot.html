How Much Do Contractors Charge for Labor Only? Hourly, Daily, and Per Square Foot

Updated September 2026 · 8 min read

Most published cost guides quote a single installed price that bundles labor and materials together. That is fine when you are budgeting a whole project, and useless when you are buying the material yourself, comparing two bids, or trying to work out whether $900 to hang a closet is reasonable. This page covers labor only: what the work costs before anything is bought.

Labor is quoted four different ways depending on the job, and the same crew will use different structures for different work. Knowing which one applies to your job is most of the battle, because a small job priced hourly and the same small job priced as a flat fee can differ by a factor of two.

The Four Ways Labor Gets Priced

StructureUsed forWhat to watch
HourlyRepairs, diagnostics, punch-list work, anything with unknown scopeMinimum call-out charges; travel time in or out
Day rateMulti-day carpentry, framing, trim-out, crews on siteUsually 8 hours; ask what happens at hour 9
Per square footPainting, flooring, tile, drywall, roofingWhich area is measured, and whether prep is included
Per linear footTrim, baseboard, crown, fencing, guttersCorners and returns often priced separately

A single job can mix them. A trim carpenter might quote baseboard per linear foot, a door install as a flat fee per door, and repairs to damaged casing at an hourly rate. That is normal and not a red flag.

Day Rates and Crew Rates

A day rate is not simply the hourly rate times eight. Contractors discount slightly for a guaranteed full day because it eliminates the scheduling gaps that make hourly work inefficient, so expect roughly 10 to 15 percent below the hourly equivalent.

TradeHourly (1 person)Day rate (1 person, 8 hr)
General contractor$50–150$360–1,080
Carpenter / framer$35–90$250–650
Trim carpenter (finish)$45–100$325–720
Electrician$50–130$360–935
Plumber$45–120$325–865
Painter$25–70$180–505
Tile setter$30–80$215–575

Crews are not priced per head at the same rate. A four-person finish crew is not four times the lead carpenter's rate, because the crew is usually one lead, one or two mid-level installers, and a helper. A realistic blended figure for a four-person finish crew is roughly $150 to $280 per hour, or $1,100 to $2,200 per day, rather than four times a lead's $90.

When a contractor quotes "a crew for two days," ask how many people and at what mix. Two skilled carpenters and two helpers produce very different output from four helpers, at a similar headline number.

Why Small Jobs Cost So Much Per Foot

This is the single most common source of sticker shock, and it is not gouging. Every job carries fixed overhead that does not shrink with the job: travel, loading and unloading, setting up and protecting the space, a supply run, and cleanup. On a two-day job that overhead is absorbed. On a two-hour job it is the job.

Job sizeTypical labor totalEffective rate
Hang one closet shelf unit (2 x 5 ft reach-in)$300–800Very high per sq ft; minimum charge dominates
Trim out one 12 x 12 room, baseboard and casing$450–900$4–7 per linear ft
Trim out a whole 1,500 sq ft house$1,800–3,500$3–5 per linear ft
Paint one 12 x 12 bedroom$300–600$1.80–3.50 per sq ft
Paint a whole interior, 1,500 sq ft house$2,200–4,500$1.50–2.50 per sq ft

Notice the direction: the per-unit rate falls as the job grows, and the published "average per square foot" figures you find online almost always describe whole-house scope. Applying a whole-house rate to one room will underestimate the quote you actually receive, often badly.

Two practical responses. First, batch small jobs. Five two-hour tasks booked together as one day are dramatically cheaper than five separate visits, because you pay the setup overhead once. Second, if a job is genuinely tiny, expect to pay the trade's minimum call-out, which is typically two to four hours for a general contractor or carpenter and one to two hours for an electrician or plumber, whether the work takes that long or not.

Pricing a Multi-Trade Scope

When a project touches several trades, the total is not one rate applied to the whole square footage. Break the scope into line items, price each on its own basis, and add a general contractor margin if someone is coordinating the trades.

Take a realistic example: remove 450 sq ft of glue-down flooring, remove and reinstall fixtures, install 500 sq ft of new hardwood, paint, repair door trim, and install new baseboard. Priced line by line, labor only:

Line itemBasisLabor only
Glue-down removal, 450 sq ft$1.50–4.00 /sq ft$675–1,800
Fixture removal and reinstallHourly or per fixture$200–600
Hardwood install, 500 sq ft$3–8 /sq ft$1,500–4,000
Interior painting$1.50–3.50 /sq ft$700–1,800
Door trim repairHourly$150–500
Baseboard install$3–5 /linear ft$450–900
Subtotal, labor only$3,675–9,600
GC coordination, 10–20% if applicable$370–1,920

The range is wide because it spans a rural single operator and an urban crew, and glue-down removal in particular can triple if the adhesive is stubborn or the subfloor needs remediation. That is why removal is the line item most often written as an allowance rather than a fixed price. If a bid gives you one number for a scope like this with no breakdown, ask for the line items; a contractor who priced it properly already has them.

Regional Adjustment

Every figure above is a national middle. Dense urban markets typically run 15 to 25 percent above these numbers, and rural markets 15 to 20 percent below. High cost-of-living metros can exceed the top of each range outright rather than sitting inside it. Adjust before you decide a quote is unreasonable, and compare bids against your local market rather than against a national average.

What About the Estimate Itself?

For most residential work, a walkthrough and a written estimate are free, and a contractor who wants payment simply to look at a straightforward job is unusual. Charges are normal in three situations: a diagnostic visit where finding the problem is the work, a detailed design or measured takeoff you keep regardless of who does the job, and a long-distance trip. Many contractors credit a paid estimate against the job if you hire them, which is worth asking about directly.

Three or four things make a bid comparable to another bid: the same scope in writing, a clear statement of what is labor and what is materials, who supplies and who disposes, and the payment schedule. Bids that differ by more than about 30 percent usually differ in scope, not in price.

The Bottom Line

For labor only, budget hourly rates for repairs and unknowns, day rates for multi-day carpentry, and per-square-foot or per-linear-foot rates for painting, flooring, tile, and trim. Expect the effective rate to be much higher on small jobs than any published average, because minimum charges and setup overhead dominate. Break multi-trade projects into line items before comparing bids, and adjust the whole thing 15 to 25 percent for a major metro or down for a rural market.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a contractor charge per day for labor?

A one-person day rate typically runs about 10 to 15 percent below eight times the hourly rate, because a guaranteed full day removes scheduling gaps. That puts a carpenter at roughly $250 to $650 per day, a general contractor at $360 to $1,080, and a painter at $180 to $505. Confirm what a day means in the quote, since some contractors count eight hours on site and others count portal to portal including travel.

How much does a 4-person crew cost per hour?

Less than four times the lead's rate, because a crew is normally one lead, one or two mid-level installers, and a helper rather than four senior tradespeople. A realistic blended figure for a four-person finish crew is about $150 to $280 per hour, or roughly $1,100 to $2,200 per day. Ask for the crew composition rather than just the headcount, since two skilled carpenters plus two helpers produce very different output from four helpers at a similar price.

Why is the per square foot price higher for a small job?

Because every job carries fixed overhead that does not shrink: travel, loading, setting up and protecting the space, a supply run, and cleanup. Spread across a whole-house job that overhead disappears into the rate. On a single room it can be most of the invoice. Published per-square-foot averages almost always describe whole-house scope, so applying them to one room will underestimate the quote you receive.

Do contractors charge for an estimate?

Usually not for straightforward residential work, where a walkthrough and a written estimate are free. Charges are normal for diagnostic visits where finding the problem is the actual work, for detailed design or a measured takeoff you keep regardless of who does the job, and for long-distance travel. Many contractors credit a paid estimate toward the project if you hire them, so it is worth asking.

How do I know if a contractor's labor price is fair?

Compare it against local rates rather than national averages, since major metros run 15 to 25 percent above the middle and rural markets 15 to 20 percent below. Then check that the bids you are comparing describe the same scope in writing, state what is labor and what is materials, and say who supplies and disposes. Bids more than about 30 percent apart usually differ in scope rather than in price, and the cheapest bid is often the one that left something out.
